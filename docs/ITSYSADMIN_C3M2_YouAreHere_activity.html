Give them what the other warehouse has

A second warehouse has just joined the network at a mid-market retailer. You are the junior systems administrator. Six new starters need access, and the request from operations is one sentence: give them what the other warehouse has.

The retailer keeps its accounts in a directory, the system that stores every account and answers who someone is. Inside it, accounts belong to groups, which are named sets of accounts that can be given access together, and sit in containers, which are named places in the directory where accounts are kept.

Three things are already on your desk. Each looks like the start of an answer.

What your reading misses

In this directory, access is granted to groups and to containers rather than to individual accounts. So one complaint about one person fits at least three different causes:

  1. The person is in no group that the access was granted to.
  2. The group that holds the access has a scope, a setting on the group that decides where its access can apply, and that scope does not reach this folder.
  3. The person's account sits in a container that the granted settings do not cover.

Nothing in the ticket tells these apart, and reading it again will not either. Fixing one person may get one starter working. It leaves you unable to say why it worked, or why the other five are still waiting.

A group carries two things: its members, and a scope, a setting on the group that decides what it may contain and where its access can apply. Membership answers who is in the group. Scope answers where the group's access can land.

A membership list shows only the first. A group with every right name on it can still grant nothing to a folder its scope cannot reach. The list cannot warn you, because scope belongs to the group itself, not to anyone listed in it.

Copying an account reproduces where it sits and which groups it belongs to. It does not reproduce the reason for either. If the original clerk still holds access left over from an old role, or authority nobody wrote down, all six starters now hold it too.

That is a finding in its own right. DISA's Active Directory hardening guidance requires that the accounts holding delegated authority over directory objects can be listed, each with a reason on record. Six copies of an unexplained account give six more entries nobody can explain.

What all three miss

Each reading answers one question: who ends up with access? The question underneath is different. Where was the access granted, and what carries it to each person?

All three items on your desk describe the last part. None of them shows the first two.

The missing capability

Saying, for any piece of access, where it was granted and what route carries it to each person, and setting that up before anyone depends on it.

Without it, you can make access work. You cannot account for it.
